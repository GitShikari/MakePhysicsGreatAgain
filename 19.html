    Torque is the rate of change of L

    Torque and force are entirely different quantities, linked by τ = r × F. Differentiating L = r × p reveals why the first product-rule term vanishes identically.

    Interactive 3D cross-product: τ = r × F

    drag to orbit
    Lever arm r sin φ
    1.36 m
    |τ| = r F sin φ
    2.72 N·m
    τ · r
    0.000
    τ · F
    0.000
    L=r×p
    Angular momentum of a particle about the chosen origin, where p = mv.
    τ=r×F=dLdt
    Torque is always mutually perpendicular to both r and F.

    Derivation: why dL/dt = τ (Slide 5)

    1. 01
      dLdt = d(r × p)dt
      Start by taking the time derivative of angular momentum L = r × p with respect to an inertial origin.
    2. 02
      = (drdt × p) + (r × dpdt)
      Apply the product rule for cross products, keeping the order of the vectors intact.
    3. 03
      = (v × mv) + (r × dpdt)
      Substitute dr/dt = v and p = mv. Since v is parallel to mv, their cross product v × mv = 0!
    4. 04
      dLdt = r × F = τ
      By Newton's second law dp/dt = F. When net external torque τ = 0, L = constant (Conservation of Angular Momentum).
    Why internal torques cancel (Slide 5). The net torque on any system equals the total external torque because the sum of all internal torques is always zero — the rotational analogue of Newton's 3rd Law. Applications include rotating bicycle/motorcycle wheels, gyroscopes, and natural phenomena like hurricanes and figure skaters.

    Torque vs. force on a disk, and why origin matters

    There can be a torque on a system with zero net force, and a force with zero net torque. And while force never cares where you place your coordinate origin, torque depends on it directly.

    Torque about the center of the disk (Slide 4)

    Net Force |F_net|
    5.0 N
    τ about Center
    5.0 N·m
    τ about Shifted Origin O
    5.0 N·m
    Motion produced
    translates + spins
    Case 1: Single tangential force. Both net force F = f and torque τ = rf are non-zero. Try shifting the origin O with the slider: the force F never changes, but the torque about O changes linearly!

    Four essential rules from Slide 4

    • Origin dependence: Torque τ = r × F depends on the origin we choose because r is measured from that origin. Force F does not.
    • Mutual perpendicularity: Torque and force are always mutually perpendicular (τ ⊥ F).
    • Independent zeroes: Case 2 (a pure couple) has Fnet = 0 but τ = 2rf. Case 3 has τ = 0 about the center because the two torques oppose, while Fnet = 2f.
    • Pure couple invariance: Switch to Case 2 (Couple) and drag the origin slider O — notice that when Fnet = 0, the torque 2rf is actually independent of the origin!

    Angular momentum of a rotating skew rod

    Angular momentum L is not necessarily parallel to angular velocity ω. Watch a massless rod of length 2l with two masses m fixed at angle α spin about the vertical z axis.

    Derivation: magnitude and direction of L (Slides 7–8)

    1. 01
      r′ = l cos α,   v = r′ω = l cos α ω
      As measured in Slide 7, α is the angle between L and the z axis (equivalently, the rod's angle above the horizontal plane). Each mass moves in a horizontal circle of radius r′ = l cos α.
    2. 02
      p = mv = mlω cos α
      Linear momentum p is tangential to the circular path (pointing into/out of the page in the co-rotating slice).
    3. 03
      L = Σ (ri × pi)  ⟹  L = 2 m ω l2 cos α
      Since r lies along the rod (length l) and is perpendicular to the tangential p, each mass contributes l p = mωl2 cos α in the same direction!
    4. 04
      Lz = L cos α,   Lh = L sin α
      Because L ⊥ r and lies in the plane of the rod and the z axis, L makes angle α with z. Its tip traces out a circle of radius L sin α about the z axis!
    Press "Co-rotating plane (Slide 8)" above. That locks the camera to rotate with the rod so you see the exact 2D textbook diagram from Slide 8: L tilted at angle α from the z axis, decomposed into vertical Lz = L cos α and horizontal Lh = L sin α.
    Slides 9–10

    Why a spinning skew rod needs a wobbling torque

    L is constant in magnitude, but its horizontal part Lh = L sin α swings around in a circle. Differentiating L(t) shows that the bearings must supply a rotating torque τ = dL/dt just to keep the axle fixed.

    Horizontal projection of L and τ = dL/dt

    left: 3D · right: τ(α) curve
    L_x = L sin α cos ωt
    0.00
    L_y = L sin α sin ωt
    0.00
    τ_x = dL_x/dt
    0.00 N·m
    τ_y = dL_y/dt
    0.00 N·m
    |τ| = √(τ_x² + τ_y²)
    2.56 N·m

    Derivation: differentiating L(t) to find τ (Slides 9–10)

    1. 01
      Lx = Lh cos ωt = L sin α cos ωt,   Ly = L sin α sin ωt
      Choose xy axes so Lh coincides with the +x axis at t = 0. As the rod turns by angle ωt, Lh sweeps in the xy plane.
    2. 02
      L = L sin α (cos ωt x + sin ωt y) + L cos α z
      Write the full 3D vector L(t). Notice that the z component is constant in time.
    3. 03
      τ = dLdt = Lω sin α (−sin ωt x + cos ωt y)
      Differentiate with respect to t. The z component disappears, and τ lies purely in the xy plane, 90° ahead of Lh!
    4. 04
      τx = −2ml2ω2 sin α cos α sin ωt,   τy = 2ml2ω2 sin α cos α cos ωt
      Substitute L = 2ml2ω cos α from Slide 7 into each component.
    5. 05
      τ = √(τx2 + τy2) = 2ml2ω2 sin α cos α = ωL sin α
      Slide 10 conclusion: Torque τ = 0 when α = 0 (rod horizontal, L ∥ ω) and when α = π/2 (masses sit right on the z axis, so L = 0). Peak dynamic imbalance occurs at α = 45°!
    Why tire balancing matters. If a car wheel is mounted slightly skewed (α ≠ 0), its bearings must exert a rotating torque 2ml2ω2 sin α cos α that grows with ω2 — felt as violent steering-wheel shimmy at highway speeds.

    Conservation of L & Hoberman sphere

    When net external torque is zero (τ = 0), total angular momentum L = Iω stays constant. Pull the mass inward to shrink I, and spin rate ω must jump up in proportion.

    Expanding / contracting Hoberman sphere & skater (Slides 11–12)

    Why kinetic energy increases when you pull your arms in

    In the two lecture videos on Slides 11 and 12 (the Hoberman sphere and the spinning figure skater), no external torque acts about the vertical axis, so L = Iω is strictly conserved. Halving the radius reduces I by a factor of 4, which forces ω to increase by a factor of 4.

    Watch the kinetic energy readout K = ½Iω2 = L2/(2I) above: when I drops by 4×, rotational kinetic energy quadruples! That extra energy comes from the internal muscular work done pulling the masses inward against the centrifugal tension.

    Slides 13–15

    From jth particle to Lz = Iω

    Every particle mj in a rigid body circles the z axis at perpendicular distance ρj = √(xj2 + yj2), not rj = √(xj2 + yj2 + zj2). Projecting onto the rotation axis gives the geometrical quantity I.

    The jth particle of a rotating body (Slides 14–15)

    drag to orbit
    Origin distance r_j
    1.63 m
    Axis distance ρ_j
    1.20 m
    Speed v_j = ρ_j ω
    1.80 m/s
    L_z(j) = m_j ρ_j² ω
    2.16 kg·m²/s
    Contribution I_j = m_j ρ_j²
    1.44 kg·m²

    Derivation: why L_z = I ω and I = ∫ ρ² dm (Slides 14–15)

    1. 01
      L(j) = rj × mjvj,   rj = (xj2 + yj2 + zj2)1/2
      The full 3D angular momentum of particle j measured from the origin is tilted if zj ≠ 0.
    2. 02
      Lz(j) = (distance to z axis) × mjvj = ρj mj vj
      Since vj lies entirely in the horizontal xy plane, the z component of rj × mjvj depends only on ρj = (xj2 + yj2)1/2! Slide the zj slider above and notice Lz(j) never changes.
    3. 03
      Lz = Σj Lz(j) = Σj mj vj ρj = (Σj mj ρj2) ω
      Substitute vj = ρjω and sum over every particle in the rigid body. Because the body is rigid, every particle shares the same ω.
    4. 04
      Lz = I ω,   I = Σj mj ρj2 = ∫ ρ2 dm
      Slide 15 definition: I is a geometrical quantity called the moment of inertia. It depends on both the distribution of mass in the body and the location of the axis of rotation.

    Moment of inertia: Thin Hoop vs. Uniform Disk

    Why does a uniform disk have exactly half the moment of inertia of a thin hoop of the same mass M and radius R? Sweep the integration element dm below to watch both integrals accumulate.

    Step-by-step integrations from Slide 16

    1. 01

      Part a — Uniform thin hoop of mass M, radius R

      dm = λ ds,   where  λ = M2πR
      Every arc segment ds on the hoop sits at the exact same perpendicular distance ρ = R from the central axis.
    2. 02
      I = ∫ ρ2 dm = R2 ∫02πR λ ds = R2 (M2πR) (2πR) = MR2
      Because R2 is constant for the entire hoop, it factors straight out of the integral.
    3. 03

      Part b — Uniform disk of mass M, radius R

      dA = 2πρ dρ  ⟹  dm = MA dA = M (2πρ dρ)πR2 = 2Mρ dρR2
      Slice the solid disk into concentric thin rings of radius ρ and thickness dρ. Each ring is a mini-hoop!
    4. 04
      dI = ρ2 dm = 2Mρ3 dρR2  ⟹  I = ∫0R 2Mρ3 dρR2 = 12MR2
      Integrating ρ3 from 0 to R yields R4/4. Multiplying by 2M/R2 gives ½MR2.

    Thin stick (midpoint vs. end) & uniform sphere

    Shifting the rotation axis of a thin stick of length L from its midpoint to one end changes the integration limits from [−L/2, +L/2] to [0, L] — quadrupling I from ML2/12 to ML2/3.

    Complete solutions for Slide 17 (a, b, and c)

    1. 01

      a. Uniform thin stick — axis through midpoint

      dm = λ dx = ML dx,   I = ∫−L/2+L/2 x2 dm = ML [x33]−L/2+L/2 = 112ML2
      Because the axis passes through the center, the furthest mass is only at distance L/2, keeping x2 small: ((L/2)3 − (−L/2)3)/3 = L3/12.
    2. 02

      b. Try yourself! — Uniform thin stick, axis at one end

      I = ML ∫0L x2 dx = ML (L33) = 13ML2
      Now x runs from 0 to L. Because x2 weights the far end heavily, pivoting at the end makes the stick 4 times harder to angularly accelerate!
    3. 03

      c. Try yourself! — Uniform solid sphere of mass M, radius R

      dI = 12 (dm) ρ2,   where  ρ2 = R2 − z2,  dm = M(4/3)πR3 π(R2 − z2) dz
      Slice the sphere into thin horizontal disks at height z of radius ρ = √(R2 − z2). By Slide 16b, each thin disk has dI = ½ (dm) ρ2.
    4. 04
      I = 3M8R3 ∫−R+R (R2 − z2)2 dz = 25MR2
      (Note: Slide 17 prints a small typo 25ML2 in the red box — for a sphere of radius R, it is 25MR2.)

    What every symbol means

    τ
    Torque about the chosen origin, r × F = dL/dt. Units N·m.
    L
    Total angular momentum vector, Σ(ri × pi). Units kg·m²/s.
    Lz, Lh
    Component of L along the rotation axis (L cos α) and horizontal component perpendicular to the axis (L sin α).
    α
    In the skew-rod problem (Slides 6–10), the fixed angle between L and the z axis (equal to the rod's angle from the horizontal plane).
    ρj
    Perpendicular distance √(xj2 + yj2) from the z axis of rotation to particle j.
    rj
    3D radial distance √(xj2 + yj2 + zj2) from the coordinate origin to particle j.
    λ
    Linear mass density (M/(2πR) for a hoop, or M/L for a uniform thin stick).
