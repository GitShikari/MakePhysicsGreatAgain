    Inertia tensor of a cube about its corner

    A uniform cube of side L and mass M sits with its edges along the x, y, z axes and one corner at the origin. Every element of [I] is a triple integral over 0 ≤ x, y, z ≤ L with density ρ = M/L3.

    Step-by-step integration (Slides 3–4)

    1. 01
      dm = ρ dx dy dz,   ρ = ML3
      The cube is uniform, so mass is spread evenly over volume L3.
    2. 02
      Ixx = ρ ∫0L∫0L∫0L (y2 + z2) dx dy dz = ρ · L · 2 · L · L33 = 23ML2
      The y2 term gives L · (L3/3) · L; the z2 term gives the same. By symmetry Iyy = Izz = 23ML2.
    3. 03
      Ixy = −ρ ∫0Lx dx ∫0Ly dy ∫0Ldz = −ρ · L22 · L22 · L = −14ML2
      All mass lies in the first octant (x, y > 0), so xy never cancels — products of inertia are non-zero. Likewise Iyz = Izx = −14ML2.
    4. 04
      [I]corner = ML22/3−1/4−1/4−1/42/3−1/4−1/4−1/42/3= ML2128−3−3−38−3−3−38
      Slide 4 result. The corner frame is not a principal frame: rotating about x gives L with y and z components too.

    Slides 15–23

    How to find the principal axis?

    If L and ω are parallel one can write L = λω. For simple geometries the principal axes are obvious by symmetry; in general we need the mathematical approach — eigenvalues and eigenvectors of [I].

    Two-step recipe

    1. 01
      [I]ω = L = λω
      Step 1 (Slide 17): impose the condition that makes L and ω parallel. Unknowns: the direction of ω and the scalar λ.
    2. 02
      [I]ω = λ[1]ω  ⟹  ([I] − λ[1])ω = 0
      Slide 18: insert a unit matrix so both sides are matrices acting on ω.
    3. 03
      det([I] − λ[1]) = 0
      Slide 19 — characteristic equation. Theorem: if [A][x] = 0 has a non-zero solution, [A] is non-invertible, so |A| = 0.
    4. 04
      λ = λ1, λ2, λ3  →  ([I] − λk[1])ωk = 0
      Slides 20–21: the cubic gives three eigenvalues; substituting each one back and solving for ω's gives the eigenvectors — the principal axes.
    5. 05
      D = P−1[I]P = λ1000λ2000λ3
      Step 2 (Slides 22–23): choose the eigenvector directions as the new coordinate axes. The diagonalisation theorem (MA102), with P = matrix of eigenvectors, rotates the axes so that the eigenvalues sit on the diagonal — the principal moments.
    Why "parallel" is the key condition. Rotation about a principal axis needs no extra torque to keep the axis fixed: L is along ω, so a body spinning steadily has constant L — no wobble.

    Eigenvalues, eigenvectors & the diagonal tensor

    For an unknown rigid body in the xy-plane we are given [I] = 2−1−12. Steer ω until L lines up with it — those directions are the eigenvectors. Change the matrix to experiment.

    Worked solution (Slides 26–34)

    1. 01
      2−1−12ωxωy= λωxωy
      Slide 26: apply the condition [I]ω = λω to the given tensor.
    2. 02
      det2 − λ−1−12 − λ= (2 − λ)2 − 1 = λ2 − 4λ + 3 = 0
      Slide 27: characteristic equation → λ1 = 1, λ2 = 3.
    3. 03
      λ1 = 1:  ωx − ωy = 0  ⟹  e1 = 1√2(1, 1)
      Slide 28: the line y = x.
    4. 04
      λ2 = 3:  ωx + ωy = 0  ⟹  e2 = 1√2(−1, 1)
      Slides 29–30: the line y = −x, perpendicular to e1 (eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix are orthogonal).
    5. 05
      D = 1003 ⟹  L1 = 1·ω1,  L2 = 3·ω2
      Slides 31–34: [I] is still not diagonal in xy. Rotating the axes by 45° onto e1, e2 writes the eigenvalues on the diagonal — the principal-axis tensor.
