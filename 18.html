    When is the axis actually fixed?

    Almost every formula in this chapter assumes fixed-axis rotation. A car wheel satisfies it only while the car drives straight ahead. Switch between the three cases and watch the ω⃗ arrow.

    A wheel, three ways

    drag to orbit
    Axis direction
    (1.00, 0.00, 0.00)
    Fixed axis?
    yes
    Valid here
    τ = Iα, L = Iω
    Driving straight. The wheel turns about the axle and nothing else. ω⃗ keeps one direction, so a single number I describes the whole body and τ = Iα holds.

    From the slide, in full: a car wheel attached to an axle undergoes fixed-axis rotation as long as the car drives straight ahead. If the car turns, the wheel must rotate about a vertical axis while simultaneously spinning on the axle — the motion is no longer fixed-axis rotation. If the wheel flies off the axle and wobbles down the road, the motion is definitely not rotation about a fixed axis.

    This matters because I is only a single number for a chosen axis. Change the axis and I changes, L stops being parallel to ω, and you need the full inertia tensor.

    Slides 5, 7, 8

    The dynamical formulas, and where they come from

    Four results do most of the work. The two on the right are the ones that trip people up, because they split a body's motion into spin about the centre of mass plus travel of the centre of mass itself.

    a. Pure rotation about a fixed axis — no translation

    L=Iω
    Angular momentum. Parallel to the axis, proportional to how fast it spins.
    τ=Iα
    Torque sets angular acceleration — Newton's second law for turning.
    K=12Iω2
    Rotational kinetic energy.
    W=∫θ₁θ₂ τ dθ=ΔK
    Work–energy theorem for rotation.

    Derivation: why ∫τ dθ equals ΔK

    1. 01
      W = ∫ F · dr
      Start from the definition of work you already have for translation. Nothing new yet.
    2. 02
      dr = (ω × r) dt
      For a rigid body turning about a fixed axis, every particle's displacement is forced by the rotation. This is the one substitution that turns a translation law into a rotation law.
    3. 03
      F · (ω × r) = ω · (r × F) = ω · τ
      Rotate the scalar triple product. The force reappears only as its torque — any part of F pointing along r does no work, which is exactly what torque already throws away.
    4. 04
      W = ∫ τ ω dt = ∫θ₁θ₂ τ dθ
      Because ω = dθ/dt. Torque × angle swept, the exact mirror of force × distance.
    5. 05
      τ = I dωdt = I ω dωdθ
      Chain rule, so the integral can be taken over ω instead of θ.
    6. 06
      W = ∫ Iω dω = 12Iω22 − 12Iω12
      The work–energy theorem for rotation. Same shape as ½mv22 − ½mv12, with the swapped symbols from the table in section 01.

    b. Rotation and translation

    Subscript 0 refers to the centre of mass. Write each particle's position as r = R + r′ — centre of mass plus a bit measured from it — and the angular momentum splits cleanly in two.

    Lz = I0ω + (R × MV)z
    Spin about the centre of mass, plus the orbital angular momentum of the centre of mass treated as one point particle.
    τz = τ0 + (R × F)z
    Torque about the centre of mass from the individual external forces, plus the torque of the total force acting at the centre of mass.
    τ = Σj (r′j × fj) + R × F
    The same split written out before taking the z component.
    K = 12I0ω2 + 12MV2
    Kinetic energy splits the same way: spin energy plus the energy of the centre of mass.
    Read the split this way. R is the position vector of the centre of mass measured from whatever origin you chose. Move the origin to the centre of mass and the orbital term vanishes — which is exactly the trick used in section 08 on the rolling wheel.

    Derivation: from torque to a sine wave

    1. 01
      τ = r × F = −lMg sin θ z
      Only the component of gravity perpendicular to the body, Mg sin θ, produces torque. The Mg cos θ component points along r and contributes nothing. The minus sign says the torque always pushes back toward θ = 0.
    2. 02
      Iα = −lMg sin θ
      Apply τ = Iα about the pivot. Note I is taken about the pivot, not the centre of mass.
    3. 03
      I d2θdt2 + Mgl sin θ = 0
      Rearranged, this is the exact equation of motion. It has no solution in elementary functions — the black curve above is this equation integrated numerically.
    4. 04
      sin θ ≈ θ  ⟹  I θ̈ + Mgl θ = 0
      The small-angle approximation. Push the release-angle slider above 30° and watch the two curves drift apart — that drift is this line failing.
    5. 05
      θ(t) = A sin ωt + B cos ωt,   ω = √MglI
      Simple harmonic motion. A and B come from how you release it; released from rest at θ0 gives A = 0, B = θ0.
    Sanity check the formula. Put a point mass at the end of a massless rod: I = Ml2, so ω = √(Mgl/Ml2) = √(g/l) — the simple pendulum, recovered. Switch the body buttons above and the I readout changes while ω = √(Mgl/I) keeps working.

    Why θ is not a vector but ω is

    This is the one result in the chapter you cannot get from a formula — you have to watch it. Rotate a box 90° about x then 90° about y, then do it in the other order. Vectors add the same either way. These don't.

    Two boxes, two orders, same angles

    drag to orbit both
    order A: x then y order B: y then x
    Mismatch between the two results
    120.0°
    Mismatch ÷ θ²
    0.0133 /deg
    Verdict
    do not commute

    Mismatch shrinks like θ2

    What the slides are claiming, line by line

    Can an angular orientation be a vector? No. There is no way to construct a vector θ = θxx + θyy + θzz that represents an orientation, because vector addition commutes and finite rotations do not:

    (θxx + θyy)  ≠  (θyy + θxx)

    Can angular velocity be a vector? Yes. Drag the angle slider down toward 1° and watch the mismatch readout collapse — it falls off as θ2, so for an infinitesimal rotation the order stops mattering at first order. Infinitesimal rotations do commute, which is exactly what makes

    ω = dθdt

    a genuine vector, even though θ itself is not. The derivative is built from infinitesimal rotations, and those add like vectors.

    The practical consequence. You may add angular velocities as vectors — a wheel spinning on an axle while the car turns has ωtotal = ωspin + ωturn. You may never add finite angles that way.

    The two dot products above stay pinned at zero no matter what you drag. That is the whole content of the cross product: the velocity is perpendicular to the plane of r and ω, which is the same as saying it is tangential to the circle the point travels on.

    Notice too that the point does not circle at radius r — it circles at radius r sin φ, its perpendicular distance from the axis. Set φ near 5° or 175° and the point nearly sits on the axis, barely moving. That sin φ is the whole reason a cross product, rather than a plain product, appears here.

    Derivation: where the cross product comes from

    1. 01
      |dr| = (r sin φ) dθ
      In time dt the body turns by dθ, and the point moves along a circle of radius r sin φ — arc length equals radius times angle.
    2. 02
      |v| = |dr|dt = ω r sin φ
      Divide by dt. The magnitude already looks like a cross product.
    3. 03
      v ⊥ ω  and  v ⊥ r
      The direction is tangential to the circle, so it is perpendicular to the plane containing r and ω. Both readouts above confirm it numerically.
    4. 04
      v = ω × r
      One vector with exactly that magnitude and exactly that direction, with the right-hand sense built in. For an infinitesimal rotation, dr = dθ × r — and this only makes sense because of section 05.

    A particle rotating in a vertical plane

    The angular velocity ω lies in the XY plane at 45° to both +X and +Y. Find the particle's velocity. Two routes — differentiate r, or take ω × r — and they appear to disagree by a sign. Resolving that is the real lesson.

    The geometry, live

    dθ/dt
    −1.00 rad/s
    |v| = ωr
    1.00
    Why the plane is vertical. The particle must move perpendicular to ω. Since ω lies flat in the XY plane along (x+y)/√2, the plane perpendicular to it is the vertical plane spanned by z and (−x+y)/√2. Press Down the axis above to look straight along ω and see that plane edge-on as a circle.

    Solution, step by step

    1. 01
      ω = ω√2(x + y)
      45° to both axes means equal x and y components, and the 1/√2 keeps the magnitude at ω. No z component — it lies flat in the XY plane.
    2. 02
      r = r (−sin θ√2x + sin θ√2y + cos θ z)
      Measure θ down from the z axis. The in-plane part has length r sin θ and points along (−x+y)/√2, so its projection on the XY plane sits in the 2nd quadrant — negative x, positive y. Check the magnitude: sin2θ/2 + sin2θ/2 + cos2θ = 1. ✓
    3. 03

      Route A — differentiate

      drdt = r(−cos θ√2x + cos θ√2y − sin θ z) dθdt
      Only θ depends on time — r is constant — so the chain rule leaves a factor dθ/dt that we have not yet evaluated.
    4. 04

      Route B — cross product

      ω × r = xyzω√2ω√20−r sin θ√2r sin θ√2r cos θ
      Top row the unit vectors, middle row ω, bottom row r. The z component gets a contribution from both terms and they add rather than cancel, which is why √2 · √2 = 2 turns into a clean sin θ.
    5. 05
      ω × r = ωr(cos θ√2x − cos θ√2y + sin θ z)
      Expand the determinant. Compare with Route A: every sign is opposite. One of them must be wrong — or an assumption is.
    6. 06
      dθdt = −ω
      The resolution. Watch the animation: with ω pointing along (x+y)/√2, the right-hand rule carries the particle toward the z axis, so θ decreases with time. The sign of dθ/dt is opposite to ω. Check it at θ = 90°: there r lies in the XY plane and ω×r = ωr z, straight up toward the z axis.
    7. 07
      v = ωr(cos θ√2x − cos θ√2y + sin θ z)
      Substituting dθ/dt = −ω into Route A flips all its signs, and the two routes agree. Magnitude: ωr√(cos2θ/2 + cos2θ/2 + sin2θ) = ωr — constant, as it must be for a particle on a circle of radius r. ✓
    The trap, stated plainly. ω × r already knows which way the body turns — the right-hand rule is baked in. When you instead differentiate a parametrisation by hand, you have to supply that information yourself through the sign of dθ/dt. Forget it and you get a velocity pointing backwards.

    Angular momentum of a rolling wheel

    A rolling wheel has two contributions, and students lose marks by counting one of them twice or not at all: the spin of the wheel about its own centre, and the orbital motion of that centre about your chosen origin.

    Rolling without slipping

    V = ωR

    −0.360
    Orbital: (R⃗ × MV⃗)_z
    −0.720
    Total L_z
    −1.080 kg·m²/s
    As a multiple of MR²ω
    −3/2

    Putting the two pieces together

    1. 01
      I0 = 12MR2
      Moment of inertia of a uniform wheel about its centre of mass, with the axis along the axle.
    2. 02
      L0 = I0ω = −12MR2ω
      Spin term. Negative because a wheel rolling in the +x direction turns clockwise when y is up and z comes out of the page, so ω points into the page, along −z. The sign is geometry, not algebra — get it from the picture.
    3. 03
      Lz = (R × MV)z = −MRV
      Orbital term. R = R y points from the ground origin up to the centre, and V = V x. Then y × x = −z, giving −MRV. Press About the centre of mass above and this term goes to zero: measured about the centre of mass, the centre of mass is not orbiting anything.
    4. 04
      V = ω × r  ⟹  V = ωR
      The rolling condition, and it is just section 06 applied at the contact point. Without slipping, the contact point is instantaneously at rest, which forces V = ωR.
    5. 05
      Lz = −12MR2ω − MR2ω = −32MR2ω
      Substitute V = ωR into the orbital term and add. The orbital part is twice the spin part — a result worth remembering, and the bars in the figure above are drawn to that scale.
    Why the origin matters but the physics doesn't. Angular momentum is always about a point. Choosing the centre of mass kills the orbital term and gives −½MR2ω; choosing the ground gives −(3/2)MR2ω. Both are correct answers to different questions. What must never change is the torque you compare it against — use the same origin for both sides of τ = dL/dt.
    Every equation in Chapter 4

    Formula sheet

    Each card names the section that shows you what it looks like. Every symbol is defined at the bottom — nothing here is left as "you know what this means".

    Angular momentum

    L = Iω
    Fixed axis, measured about a point on that axis.

    Newton's second law for rotation

    τ = Iα = dLdt
    Same origin on both sides, always.

    Torque from a force

    τ = r × F
    Only the component of F perpendicular to r counts.

    Moment of inertia

    I = Σj mj rj2 = ∫ r2 dm
    rj is the perpendicular distance from the axis, not from the origin.

    Rotational kinetic energy

    K = 12Iω2
    Add ½MV2 if the body also travels.

    Work–energy theorem

    W = ∫θ₁θ₂ τ dθ = 12Iω22 − 12Iω12
    Derived in full in section 03.

    Rigid-body pendulum

    Iθ̈ + Mgl sin θ = 0
    θ(t) = A sin ωt + B cos ωt,  ω = √(Mgl/I)
    The second line needs sin θ ≈ θ. I is about the pivot.

    Angular velocity is a vector

    ω = dθdt = ωxx + ωyy + ωzz
    Valid because infinitesimal rotations commute. θ alone is not a vector.

    Velocity of any point

    v = ω × r,   |v| = ωr sin φ
    Perpendicular to both; r sin φ is the circle's radius.

    Rotation plus translation

    Lz = I0ω + (R × MV)z
    τz = τ0 + (R × F)z
    Spin about the CM plus orbit of the CM. Subscript 0 means "about the centre of mass".

    Rolling without slipping

    V = ωR,   Lz = −32MR2ω
    The second form is about the ground origin, for a uniform wheel.

    Common moments of inertia

    point mass at l:  I = Ml2rod about its end:  I = ML2/3disc about its centre:  I = MR2/2disc about its rim:  I = 3MR2/2
    The last two differ by MR2 — the parallel-axis theorem.

    What every symbol means

    ω
    Angular velocity. Direction along the rotation axis by the right-hand rule, magnitude in rad/s.
    I
    Moment of inertia about the chosen axis — rotation's version of mass. Units kg·m².
    L
    Angular momentum about the chosen origin. Units kg·m²/s.
    τ
    Torque about the chosen origin, r × F. Units N·m.
    α
    Angular acceleration, dω/dt. Units rad/s².
    r
    Position vector of a particle from the origin.
    R
    Position vector of the centre of mass from the origin.
    r′
    Position of a particle measured from the centre of mass, so r = R + r′.
    V
    Velocity of the centre of mass.
    l
    Distance from pivot to centre of mass, in the pendulum.
    φ
    Angle between ω and r, so r sin φ is the distance to the axis.
    subscript 0
    "About the centre of mass" — I0, L0, τ0.
