    What Euler's equations are about

    In the inertial frame the inertia tensor [I] changes continuously as the rigid body tumbles. By locking our coordinate axes (x, y, z) to the body's principal axes, Ix, Iy, Iz stay constant — and the rotating unit vectors change according to dx/dt = ω × x.

    Complete stability analysis (Slide 4)

    Bound = Stable,   Unbound = Unstable
    The solution of Euler's equations reveals whether a small perturbation around a principal axis stays bounded (oscillatory) or grows exponentially.

    Applications (Slide 5)

    Aerospace Dynamics: Attitude control of satellites, missiles, and aircraft in 3D space.
    Gyroscopes & Spinning Tops: Precession, nutation, and navigation systems.

    Principal-axis increments (Slides 6–8)

    ΔLx = IxΔωx,  ΔLy = IyΔωy,  ΔLz = IzΔωz
    At t = 0 the inertial and body frames coincide, while the body-fixed unit vectors rotate with ω.

    Slides 9–13 · Derivation

    Deriving Euler's equations of motion

    Differentiating L = Lxx + Lyy + Lzz in the inertial frame produces two contributions: the rate of change inside the body frame (dL/dt)body plus the transport term ω × L from the rotating unit vectors.

    Step-by-step derivation (Slides 9–12)

    1. 01
      dLdtinertial = Lxdxdt + Lydydt + Lzdzdt + xdLxdt + ydLydt + zdLzdt
      Slide 9: apply the product rule to L = Lxx + Lyy + Lzz.
    2. 02
      dLdtinertial = ω × L + dLdtbody
      Slide 9: substitute dx/dt = ω × x (and similarly for y, z).
    3. 03
      dLdtinertial = xyzωxωyωzIxωxIyωyIzωz+ Ixωxx + Iyωyy + Izωzz
      Slide 10: choose principal axes so Lx = Ixωx, Ly = Iyωy, Lz = Izωz with constant principal moments Ix, Iy, Iz.
    4. 04
      τx = Ixωx + (Iz − Iy)ωzωy,   τy = Iyωy + (Ix − Iz)ωxωz,   τz = Izωz + (Iy − Ix)ωyωx
      Slides 11–12: expand the determinant components and equate (dL/dt)inertial = τ. Cyclic permutation (x → y → z) generates all three equations!

    Slides 14–17 & 23 · Torque-Free Stability

    Stability of rotational motion under small perturbations

    Suppose a torque-free rigid body (τ = 0) spins steadily about the x-axis (ωx ≈ const) and receives a tiny perturbation ωy, ωz ≪ ωx. Differentiating the second Euler equation and substituting the third yields a second-order ODE for ωy(t).

    Linearized perturbation equation (Slides 15–16)

    1. 01
      Ixωx + (Iz − Iy)ωzωy = 0  ⟹  Ixωx ≈ 0  ⟹  ωx ≈ constant
      Slide 15: since both ωy and ωz are small perturbations, their product ωyωz is second-order small (≈ 0).
    2. 02
      Iyd2ωydt2 + (Ix − Iz)ωxdωzdt = 0,   where  dωzdt = −Iy − IxIzωxωy
      Slide 15: differentiate the 2nd Euler equation with respect to t and substitute dωz/dt from the 3rd Euler equation.
    3. 03
      d2ωydt2 + (Ix − Iz)(Ix − Iy)IyIzωx2 ωy = 0   ⟺   d2ωydt2 + A ωy = 0
      Slides 15–16: the sign of A = (Ix − Iz)(Ix − Iy)IyIzωx2 completely determines whether the perturbation oscillates or blows up!
    4. 04
      • Ix largest or smallest ⟹ A > 0 ⟹ ωy(t) = C1cos(√A t) + C2sin(√A t)  [Bound / Stable]
      • Ix intermediate ⟹ A < 0 ⟹ ωy(t) = C1e√|A|t + C2e−√|A|t  [Unbound / Unstable]
      Slides 16 & 23: valid for small perturbations — spinning about the largest or smallest principal moment is stable; spinning about the intermediate moment is unstable!

    Slides 18–22 · Example 1

    Stability of a book tossed about its three axes

    Toss a rectangular book (principal moments I1 < I2 < I3) spinning about each of its three orthogonal axes with a tiny initial wobble. About the longest axis (smallest I) and shortest face axis (largest I) it spins cleanly — about the intermediate axis it flips wildly (the Tennis Racket / Dzhanibekov effect)!

    Important limitation (Slide 23). The linear stability equations d2ωy/dt2 ± |A|ωy = 0 are derived by dropping the product ωyωz, so the exponential growth formula is valid for small perturbations only. Once ωy, ωz become comparable to ωx, the full nonlinear Euler equations take over and make the book flip periodically while conserving total |L| and rotational kinetic energy!
